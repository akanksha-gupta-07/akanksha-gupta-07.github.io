Summary

My recommendation is to treat hiring as a programme built on three bets: a right-sized hiring engine, a quality-of-hire system, and a returnship to reach experienced talent that competitors overlook.

What this proposal shows

  • Turning a business goal into a workforce and capacity plan
  • Testing both hiring and workplace causes before prescribing fixes
  • Making trade-offs between speed, cost and quality explicit
  • Running delivery week to week on funnel ratios, SLAs and contingency triggers
  • Stress-testing targets and planning how the plan flexes
  • Designing an inclusive talent programme with a business case
  • Holding the line with clients and managers using data

01The scenario

Company X, a 4,000-person services firm, has won a client contract that needs a new delivery hub fully staffed in 6 months. The CHRO's brief: "Hit the number, but don't build a revolving door."

  • Volume and mix: 120 roles across four role families, from entry-level associates to 10 managers.
  • Legacy attrition: at the existing site, 35% of new hires leave within 12 months.
  • Agency dependence: 30% of current hires come through agencies, at roughly three times the cost of direct hires.
  • A thin local market: strong competition for experienced specialists and team leads in the hub city.
  • Starting from zero: no local recruiters, no employer brand in the city, and hiring managers who are not yet hired themselves.

02How I framed the problem

The obvious problem is speed. The bigger risk is quality: at 35% attrition, the hub would lose about 40 of its 120 hires in year one and need to hire them again.

Three questions I would answer before choosing any tactic

  1. How much hiring capacity do 120 hires in 6 months actually need, and in what order?
  2. Why does the existing site lose so many new hires, and which causes would follow us to the hub?
  3. Where can we find experienced people that competitors are not already fighting over?

TA can fix the hiring causes directly. The workplace causes need operations, HR and rewards. My job there is to bring the evidence early, because better hiring cannot make up for a broken shift pattern or uncompetitive pay.

My fixes are conditional playbooks. The quality-of-hire system and the returnship below are options prepared in advance. I would choose, size and calibrate them once the 30-day data is in, not before.

05Workforce and capacity plan

My estimate is that the hub needs about 5.7 recruiter FTEs. I would size the team from demand, not from a headcount guess.

Proposed team

  • 4 recruiters: 2 on volume hiring, 1 on specialists, 1 on leaders and team leads
  • 1 sourcer, building candidate pipelines 4–6 weeks ahead of demand
  • 1 coordinator for scheduling and offers, freeing about 15% of recruiter time

Allowing for ramp-up

Recruiters hired in the prep month will not be at full speed in month 1. I would cover the gap three ways:

  • Fill 2 of the 4 recruiter roles with experienced recruiters seconded from the existing site for the first 3 months, so the team starts with people who know the business.
  • Plan new recruiters at 50% productivity in month 1 and full productivity from month 2, which matches when volume hiring begins.
  • Use the search firm for the 4 most senior roles from day one, so leadership hiring does not wait for the team.

The productivity figures are common rules of thumb; I would replace them with the company's own data in the first month.

Notice periods of 30–90 days for experienced hires mean specialist and leader offers must be made by month 4 to join by month 6. I would track offer-to-joining drop-off as closely as offer acceptance, with weekly keep-warm contact during notice periods.

Bottleneck thresholds

  • Pipeline coverage: at least 3 shortlisted candidates per open role at interview stage
  • Aging requisitions: amber at 30 days open, red at 45 days
  • Stage time: any candidate waiting more than 5 working days at one stage is flagged

Hiring-manager SLAs, agreed before roles open: CV feedback within 48 hours, interview slots within 5 working days, and a decision within 24 hours of the final round. When a manager is consistently slow, I would offer trained panel substitutes before escalating.

Protecting the team at peak: a cap on active requisitions per recruiter, one interview-free day a week, and rotating recruiters off the hardest roles every 6–8 weeks.

The quality-of-hire scorecard I would track monthly, alongside speed

  • Hiring-manager satisfaction at 90 days (1–5)
  • New-hire performance rating at 6 months
  • Retention at 90 days and 12 months
  • Time to full productivity

09Returnship programme

A returnship would turn the hardest gap, experienced specialists and team leads in a thin market, into an advantage. It hires professionals returning after a career break of 2+ years, for caregiving, relocation or other reasons.

Why I would bet on it

  • Returners bring 5–15 years of experience, and few employers compete for them because many screen out CV gaps.
  • They are less exposed to poaching in their first year, which supports the retention goal.
  • It gives a new hub a distinctive employer-brand story in a city where no one knows the company.

Proposed design

ElementDesign choice
FormatPaid 16-week programme in a real role, not a training course
Cohort16–24 returners for specialist and team-lead roles, sized to the number of ready host managers
TimingSource and select during the prep month; cohort starts at the beginning of month 1; week 16 falls at the end of month 4, leaving a full month to backfill any gaps before the deadline
HostingOnly managers in seat by month 1 host returners, at most 4 each; experienced staff from the existing site act as remote mentors, so new managers are not overloaded during ramp-up
SourcingReturner communities, LinkedIn's career-break feature, a "careers after a break" event
SelectionSkills-based assessment; the CV gap is never treated as a negative
FlexibilityHybrid work and a phased start (3 days a week for the first 4 weeks)

Manager readinessA 60-minute briefing for every host manager
ConversionA check-in at week 8; early offers from week 12 for strong performers; final decisions at week 16 against the same job scorecard as other hires; target 75%

Condition to proceed: I would launch the returnship only if the 30-day checks confirm enough returner interest and at least 4 managers ready to host by month 1. If not, I would run a smaller pilot and shift those roles to direct sourcing.

The business case I would put to finance: one returner should cost less than one agency hire, and every returner who stays avoids the cost of hiring again.

106-month roadmap

I would run hiring as a programme: a prep month to build the team and tools, managers first, then monthly waves of volume hiring. Sourcing returners in the prep month lets the cohort start in month 1 and finish by the end of month 4, leaving a month to backfill any gaps.

14What I would validate in the first 30 days

This plan rests on assumptions. Before committing budget, I would test the ones that would change the plan most if wrong.

  • Attrition causes: do exit data and hiring records support the hypotheses above?
  • Recruiter productivity: do the company's own numbers match the rules of thumb in the capacity plan?
  • Local market: salary benchmarks and talent supply for specialists in the hub city
  • Returner demand: interest from returner communities, and host managers willing to take part
  • Hiring-manager buy-in: 1:1 conversations with the first managers about scorecards and panels
  • Tools: whether the current applicant tracking system can report quality-of-hire data

I would also ask operations and rewards for shift, overtime and pay data, since the workplace hypotheses cannot be tested from TA data alone.
